Independence

Advertising and Independence

Advertising and sponsorship buy space and time. They do not buy coverage. No advertiser, sponsor, donor, or institutional subscriber reviews, approves, shapes, or vetoes a story, and none sees a story before it runs.

Sponsored content, if it ever runs, is labeled as such in a way no reader could miss, and is not written by our editorial staff. Anyone who presses us about content is referred to the editor, and the exchange may itself be reported.

Where a contributor introduced a sponsor, that relationship is recorded internally and disclosed in the piece if it touches the subject.

Accountability

If we get something wrong, we say so in print and online. Every issue carries the corrections stamp whether or not there are corrections to run. Online, the correction appears on the story itself with the date and what changed. We do not quietly edit the record.

To report an error, write to info@dbw-news.com with the story, the passage, and what you say is wrong. We answer every request. Where a person is criticized in a story, we seek their response before publication and print it fairly.

We do not remove published work on request. In narrow cases, such as a safety risk, a legal requirement, or a subject who was a minor, we will consider an update, an unpublishing note, or a removal, and the decision rests with the editor.

Some of what we publish is paid and some of it is not, and the difference is set before you write rather than after. Every submission carries a declaration that the work is original and human-written, and it is a term of the contribution rather than a formality. A piece found to be machine-written is killed and no fee is paid; a second one ends the working relationship. Where a contributor brings in a sponsor, the 10 percent share and its three-month qualifying period are set out in the contributor terms and paid only while the sponsor stays current.

You indemnify us against claims arising from work you submitted that turns out not to be yours, not to be true, or not to be cleared. Payment for a commissioned piece is made within thirty days of filing, whether or not we run it on the month we planned to.

Machine Writing

AI Policy

We do not publish machine-written journalism. No story here is drafted by a machine, rewritten by one, or polished by one. Every submission carries a declaration that the work is original and human-written, that declaration is a term of the contribution, and a piece found to be machine-written is killed without a fee. Work discovered to be machine-generated after publication is removed, the byline is retired, and the correction runs.

We do not publish AI-generated or AI-altered images or audio as though they were real, and we label any illustration that is not a photograph.

Where we do use machines, and why we say so. A rule that pretends we touch no software would be a lie within a month, so here is the whole list. Machines may translate what a person has already written and edited. They may transcribe our podcast and caption our video. They may suggest a headline or a summary, which an editor then writes herself rather than accepting. They may read data or a long document to help a reporter find what to look at. They may flag a spelling or a grammar problem for a person to decide about. That is the list, and anything not on it needs the editor in chief's agreement before it is used.

Two things hold across all of it. A machine never has the last word on a published sentence: a person decides, and a person is answerable for it. And a machine is never a source — nothing a model says is a fact until a reporter has confirmed it against a document or a human being who will stand behind it.

Translation is the one place machine text reaches you directly. Our reporting is written and edited in English. Where you read this site in another language, the translation is done by your browser or by a translation service, not by a person at this newsroom, and it can be wrong in ways we cannot see. Anything we print in another language is edited by a person who speaks it before it goes to press. If a translation has changed the sense of something that matters, write to corrections@dbw-news.com in whatever language you are comfortable in, quote the sentence as you read it, and we will fix it and say what happened.

Prizes

Contests, Raffles, and Auctions

Three different things, governed by three different sets of law, and we keep them apart. A giveaway is free to enter and no purchase ever improves your chance of winning. A raffle is a ticket you buy, which in Georgia requires a licence from the sheriff of the county where it is held, so no DBW raffle exists until that licence is issued and its number appears on every ticket. An auction is a purchase, and each lot's fair market value is published before bidding opens so you know what part of a winning bid is a gift and what part is not.

Directors, officers, staff, contractors and their households cannot win a prize or a raffle, and may bid at auction only at or above the published value. A prize is taxable income to the person who wins it. The full terms, including eligibility, odds, how winners are drawn and what happens if a prize fails, are on the official rules page, and any particular event's dates and prizes are published with it.

Nonpartisan

Nonpartisanship and Political Activity

As a 501(c)(3) public charity we are prohibited from intervening in political campaigns, and we take the prohibition literally. DBW News does not endorse, oppose or rate candidates for any office, does not contribute money or anything of value to a campaign or a party, and does not lend its name, list, premises or platform to one. No staff member speaks for the newsroom on a candidate's behalf, and campaign advertising is not accepted.

We do cover politics, and we do it to the same standards as anything else: issues, records, votes and consequences, with candidates and officials given a fair chance to answer. Where we hold a position on a policy — on maternal health, on housing, on public records — we say so as the newsroom's view of a policy, never as a preference between the people running for office.

Voter guides, candidate questionnaires and debates, if we run them, are open to every qualified candidate in a race on identical terms, and the questions are published with the answers.
